Rules and permits

Where am I allowed to put a tiny house?

One call to your building office answers most of it, and we will prepare you for that call.

More people ask us this than anything else, and we have been through it in fifteen countries, so we can hand you the shape of it in five minutes. The details come from your municipality, but the questions are the same everywhere and knowing them in the right order saves a great deal of wasted effort.

Four questions decide it, and it's worth asking them in this order.

1. Does your plot allow a dwelling at all? That's the local land-use plan: detaljplan, Bebauungsplan, PLU, omgevingsplan, PGOU, PDM. It has nothing to do with our tiny houses, and it's the question that stops most projects. Answer it before you choose a model, and definitely before you buy land.

2. Is a tiny house on a trailer a vehicle or a building? Almost nowhere does this turn on whether it has wheels. It turns on permanence, and on whether it's connected to mains services. Portugal's national guidance says so in as many words. Italy, Spain and Flanders have all decided that a mobile home lived in as a dwelling is a building. The practical rule: stay road-legal, stay unconnected and stay temporary, and you're usually a vehicle. Level it on supports, plumb it in and live in it, and you're a building.

3. If it's a building, which energy standard applies, and is there a way round it? Most countries exempt small or temporary buildings, usually at 50 m² or at a fixed number of years. Whether your model meets the standard without an exemption depends on the class, so have a look at the insulation question.

4. What's the transport permit threshold? 2.55 m wide and 4.0 m high is the EU-wide norm. Above that you need an exceptional-transport permit.

You ask your municipality about 1 and 2. Ask us about 3 and 4, and we'll send you drawings, weights, U-values and the technical file to take to your application.

And the single most useful thing you can do is ring the building office before you order anything. Customers who do that almost never get stuck later, and we're very happy to help you prepare for that call.

Can I live in it permanently?

Yes, with the right model, and plenty of our customers do. Which model matters more than most people expect, so this one is worth two minutes of your time.

Our mobile models under 3.5 tonnes are built light: 8 cm of insulation in the walls and 10 cm in the roof, which gives roughly U 0.44 for the wall and U 0.42 for the roof. That's the price of being towable, and it's well short of what most countries ask for year-round housing. Norway's TEK17, for example, wants 0.22 for a wall. They make excellent holiday and secondary homes, but we'd rather tell you that now than after you've ordered.

For a permanent home, look at River, Sky, Zenith, Lucia, Evergreen or Evergreen XL. These are a different build: 3-pane windows where the mobile models have 2-pane, and a great deal more insulation. Take our River as the worked example. It has been calculated for Germany at wall U 0.150, roof U 0.150, floor U 0.170 and windows Uw 0.870, and it passed comfortably.

They aren't certified to any particular efficiency scheme like KfW 55. There's a certification jungle across Europe with at least one scheme per country, so tell us which one you have to satisfy and we'll tell you honestly whether we can meet it.

Can I tow the tiny house myself?

Under 3.5 tonnes it goes behind your car. Tell us early and we build to it.

Yes, and it is one of the nicer things about our mobile models. Under 3.5 tonnes loaded, a tiny house travels behind a decent car at up to 80 km/h on a BE licence almost anywhere in Europe. You can move house without hiring anybody.

The one thing we ask is that you tell us early. Weight is something we control at the specification stage, so if towing it yourself matters to you, say so before you choose the model and we build to the limit. Get that right at the start and the rest takes care of itself.

Every one of our mobile models is built for that limit. Nature Pod, Sofia, Moon, Felicia, Luna Office, Luna Home, Maya, Sunshine, Venus, Porto and Elise are all specified up to 3.5 tonnes, so the question is never whether a model can tow, only how much room the specification leaves you.

The small and medium models have far more headroom, which is why a Sofia, a Moon, a Luna, a Maya or a Sunshine comes in comfortably with everything fitted.

If the one you have fallen for sits close to the line, there are three ways through and we will walk you through all of them: specify it lighter, let some of the furniture follow separately, or send it on a low-loader and tow nothing at all.

Our semi-mobile models are a different idea altogether. Width and height are worth a word too. 2.55 m wide and 4.0 m high is the normal legal maximum across the EU. Smile, Julia and Sky are 3.00 m and 3.45 m wide and always need an exceptional-transport permit, which we arrange as part of the delivery.

How is the trailer registered, and what papers do I get?

You get the trailer's vehicle papers and its CoC document. Together with the purchase contract, which is your proof of ownership, that's what you take to the vehicle authority in your country.

We can't send you these before you order, unfortunately. They belong to one specific trailer with its own identification number, and we buy trailers per order rather than keeping stock.

Tiny houses leave us on Polish or Latvian plates, depending on which workshop built yours, since that is where they are built. In Sweden you re-register them yourself by notifying customs and Transportstyrelsen, or we can handle it for you for an extra fee, which includes booking the inspection at the border. In Germany the tiny house goes to TÜV, and how it gets classified there, caravan or special-purpose trailer or special vehicle, varies from one test centre to the next. If you tell us which centre you're planning to use, we'll send you everything we have.

An inspector will sometimes ask for proof that the glazing is road legal. It is safety glass and we supply the manufacturer's documentation for your specific house, which normally settles it. Sunshine can also be ordered with fully road-proof windows if you want the question closed before it comes up.

Is the tiny house CE certified?

Every part inside carries CE. A hand-built house is not the kind of product that can.

Everything inside it is: the electrical parts, the plumbing, the structural components all carry their own CE certification, and the systems are built to the relevant EU directives. The structure as a whole is a different matter, and it is worth two minutes of your time, because the question usually means somebody is about to ask you for a certificate that cannot exist.

CE marking applies to products. A caravan rolling off a production line can carry one. A house built by hand is not that kind of product, so there is no CE mark for the structure as a whole, from us or from anyone building the same way.

If an authority asks you for a CE certificate for the house, tell us. It usually means they are treating it as a vehicle, and what actually helps is the technical file: drawings, weights, U-values and the component documentation. We'll send it.

The house itself

How well insulated are the tiny houses?

Mobile models: wall U 0.44. River, built for Germany: wall U 0.150.

Here are the real numbers rather than a description, because this is the question where vague answers cost people money. Insulation is where the weight is, so it differs by class.

Mobile models. 8 cm in the walls, 10 cm in the roof, and an 8 cm PIR sandwich panel in the floor. The documented values are roughly wall U 0.44, roof U 0.42, floor U 0.25 W/(m²K). That's comfortable for year-round use in Scandinavia, but short of what most countries require for a permanent dwelling.

Semi-mobile and non-mobile models. Much more. Lucia is built on 145 mm timber studs, Evergreen XL on 20 cm of mineral wool. Our River has been calculated for Germany at wall U 0.150, roof U 0.150, floor U 0.170, windows Uw 0.870, giving H'T 0.281 against a limit of 0.4222, so comfortably inside.

The material has changed. We have stopped offering recycled textile insulation, so it's mineral wool across the range now. The Nature Pod is the exception and still uses recycled textile or sheep wool.

One honest note about those numbers. The measured U-values for the mobile models were established on the recycled textile build that mineral wool has just replaced. Mineral wool of the type used has a thermal conductivity of about 0.033 to 0.035 W/(mK) against 0.039 for the textile, so at the same thickness the new build should actually be slightly better, somewhere around 0.40 rather than 0.44 for the wall. Until we have a certified sheet for the mineral wool build we'll keep quoting the older, more conservative figures, because a number you can document beats a number you calculated.

For Denmark we've used PIR board in walls and roof with a thicker sandwich panel in the floor. For Germany, wood fibre at 20 cm in the walls and 25 cm in the roof with an Intello vapour membrane instead of PE.

Thin walls don't mean a cold tiny house. The volume is small, so it heats quickly and holds its temperature, and our customers across Scandinavia tell us their houses are comfortable in midwinter. But do be honest with yourself about whether you need a permanent-residence specification, because that really is a different build, and it's much easier to decide now than later.

How do I heat the tiny house?

Most of the ways there are, and we will help you pick the one that suits how you will actually use the house.

An air-to-air heat pump heats efficiently and cools in summer, and on the larger models you might want two. Electric underfloor heating is our standard because it's light, which matters on the mobile models, and on the bigger models water-based underfloor heating works too. Infrared panels, and an infrared mirror panel in the bathroom, are popular as a supplement. A wood or pellet stove gives you lovely radiant heat and works off-grid, and we can also just prepare the flue for a stove you supply yourself.

Two practical points, and the first is a safety one rather than advice. Underfloor heating must never be covered by rugs, carpets, mattresses or furniture without legs. Covering it traps the heat and creates a burn risk. So plan the layout around it, and if you're not sure whether a piece of furniture is a problem, send us the plan and we'll look.

And a wood stove on its own isn't enough for a house left unattended in winter. When the fire goes out the house cools, so pair it with something thermostatic.

What happens if the tiny house stands empty in winter?

Leave it above 5 °C and a little warmer than outside. That's the whole trick.

Nothing at all, as long as it's kept above 5 °C and a little warmer than the air outside. Most of our customers set a thermostat low and forget about it until spring.

The reason is frozen pipes and the water damage that follows. A tiny house held at a steady temperature and humidity simply lasts longer.

The "always above outside" part matters in the shoulder seasons rather than in summer. If the house is colder than the air around it, moisture condenses inside the structure.

If a wood stove is your only heat source, add a small thermostatic electric heater so the house doesn't cool while you're away. And if you'd rather drain the system for the winter, tell us and we'll go through it with you step by step.

Off-grid

Can the tiny house be fully off-grid?

Yes, for electricity, heat and water. Plan it from the start and we build it in.

Yes, for electricity, heat and water, as long as you plan for it from the start.

Some things can be added to a finished tiny house later. What cannot be retro-fitted is the part that lives inside the structure: the pipework, the tanks and the wiring all go in before the walls are closed up. So a ready-built tiny house from the in-stock list cannot be turned into a full off-grid one afterwards. If off-grid matters to you, say so before the build rather than after, and ask us what can still be added if you have already bought.

The realistic recipe is to move the big energy consumers off electricity. Heat with a wood, pellet or gas stove. Run the boiler, hob and oven on LPG. Then a modest solar array plus a battery bank covers lighting and the small loads. Trying to run electric heating, an electric water heater and an oven off a small solar system just doesn't work, and we'd rather say so than sell you a system that disappoints you.

Do expect the off-grid equipment to add weight, which matters if you also want to stay under 3.5 tonnes. It usually can't be both.

One planning point worth knowing: in several countries, being unconnected to mains services is exactly what keeps you legally a vehicle rather than a building. Portugal says so explicitly, and France's résidence démontable category requires autonomy from networks. So going off-grid can simplify your permit position as well as your bills.

How much solar do I need?

Size it for your darkest month, not your annual total. That is the number that changes across Europe.

Start from what you will actually use, not from a number of panels. Then size the array for your worst month rather than your average one, because a system that works in June and fails in December is the usual way this goes wrong.

First, your consumption. A Swedish apartment uses roughly 2,000 to 4,000 kWh a year for household electricity. In a small, well-planned tiny house with LED lighting and with heating, hot water and cooking on something other than electricity, you can get down to roughly 600 to 1,000 kWh a year. Every big electric load you keep is worth several panels, so this is where the decision is really made.

Then your latitude, which matters more than anything else on this page. The same array on the same roof produces very different amounts in Lagos and in Lapland, and the gap is far wider in December than across the year as a whole. In northern Scandinavia there is a stretch of midwinter where no sensible array on a tiny house roof will keep up, whatever you spend; in Portugal or southern Spain the winter dip is mild enough that a modest system runs all year. Between those, in Germany, Denmark, the Netherlands or Ireland, midwinter is the pinch point but it is a manageable one.

Rather than quote a number for your country, get your own: the European Commission's free PVGIS tool gives month-by-month output for your exact coordinates, roof angle and orientation. Put your site in, look at December, and size from that. We are happy to go through the result with you.

Then the roof you actually have. On a mobile model the roof is the hard limit, and it is small. That, not the budget, is usually what caps the array. It is also why orientation and shade matter so much here: a tiny house sited in the trees, which is where people want them, can lose a large part of its winter production to shading that a rooftop system on a house would never see. Tell us where the house will stand and which way it will face.

Then storage. You need an inverter to get 230 V, and a battery bank sized for roughly three days without production. Our standard package is 1280 W of panels with 220 Ah of storage, which suits an energy-efficient tiny house used through the lighter half of the year. Batteries also lose usable capacity in the cold, so a bank that is comfortable in September is tighter in January. Keep the battery inside the insulated envelope if you can.

And be honest about midwinter. Most people living off-grid year-round in the north have a second source for the darkest weeks: a generator, an occasional shore-power connection, or simply using less. That is normal and not a failure of the system. Planning for it is cheaper than buying the array that would avoid it.

One thing to be clear about: our solar systems are off-grid systems with a battery. Excess energy is not fed back into the grid. Once the battery is full, the system simply holds it there.

Send us your location, your model and a rough list of what you want to run, and we will size it with you rather than selling you a package and hoping.
